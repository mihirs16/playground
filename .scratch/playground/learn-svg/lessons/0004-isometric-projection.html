Isometric projection — faking 3D with one formula

The win: understand and play with the single function that turns a 3D point (x,y,z) into a 2D screen point — the core illusion behind your whole exploded-axonometric prototype.

1. The whole trick is one function

There is no 3D engine. Depth is faked by mapping every 3D grid point to the screen with:

screenX = OX + (x − y) · A
screenY = OY + (x + y) · B − z
  • x, y = position on the flat ground; z = height.
  • (x − y) drives horizontal position (things spread left/right).
  • (x + y) drives how far back & down a point sits.
  • − z lifts height up the screen (remember Lesson 1: smaller y = higher).
  • OX, OY just centre the drawing in the view.

2. Where A and B come from (the viewing angle)

A and B encode the tilt of your imaginary camera:

A = S · cos(angle)      B = S · sin(angle)   // classic iso: angle = 30°

S is an overall scale (zoom). At 30° the grid gets that familiar technical-drawing look. Change the angle and the ground tilts flatter or steeper — the slider below lets you feel it.

KEY IDEA — This is an orthographic / parallel projection: parallel lines stay parallel, nothing shrinks with distance, there's no vanishing point. That's what makes it read as a blueprint, not a photograph. Adding a vanishing point (perspective) would require dividing by depth — a different pipeline.

3. Play with it

A wireframe cube sitting on a ground tile. Drag the sliders and watch the formula's inputs change live. Angle tilts the camera; rotation is Lesson 3's turntable (applied to x,y before projecting); height stretches z.

Notice: this is immediate-mode rendering — every slider move calls svg.innerHTML='' and redraws everything from scratch. Your prototype does the same thing every animation frame. (Building nodes once and only updating them is "retained mode" — a later optimisation.)

4. What we deliberately left out

This is a wireframe — I skipped solid faces on purpose. The moment you fill faces, you must decide which face is in front of which: the painter's algorithm (draw far→near) and its rotation pitfall. That's a concept worth its own lesson — it's §2 of your concepts doc, and where the "hollow boxes under rotation" bug lives.
