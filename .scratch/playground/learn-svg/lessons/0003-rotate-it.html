Rotating SVG · Lesson 3

Rotate it — the 2D rotation math

The win: derive the exact formula your prototype uses to spin the building (rx = CX + dx·cos − dy·sin), and understand why it computes rotation by hand instead of just using rotate().

0. A 90-second sin/cos refresher

Picture a point on a circle of radius 1, centred at the origin. As you sweep an angle θ anticlockwise from the +x axis, that point's coordinates are (cos θ, sin θ). That's the entire definition you need:

  • cos θ = how far right the point is (the x-coordinate).
  • sin θ = how far up the point is (the y-coordinate, in normal math axes).
  • At θ=0: (1, 0). At θ=90°: (0, 1). At 180°: (−1, 0).

Radians, not degrees. JS's Math.cos/Math.sin take radians. A full turn (360°) = 2π radians. Convert: rad = deg × Math.PI / 180. Burn that one line in — you'll type it constantly.

1. Rotating a point

To rotate a point (dx, dy) about the origin by angle θ, the new point is:

rx = dx·cos θ − dy·sin θ
ry = dx·sin θ + dy·cos θ

You don't need to memorise the derivation today — just notice its shape: each new coordinate mixes both old coordinates, weighted by cos and sin. Compare it to the prototype's turntable code — it's the same formula, just with the site centre added back:

rx = CX + dx*cos(ROT) - dy*sin(ROT)
ry = CY + dx*sin(ROT) + dy*cos(ROT)   // dx,dy = point relative to centre

2. Rotating about a pivot: the translate→rotate→translate dance

The formula above spins about (0,0). To spin about a pivot (CX,CY) you do three steps — which is exactly what those two lines encode:

  1. Translate so the pivot becomes the origin: dx = x − CX, dy = y − CY.
  2. Rotate with the formula.
  3. Translate back: add CX, CY to the result.

This is Lesson 2's pivot idea, now in numbers.

3. Two ways to rotate in SVG — and why the prototype picks the hard one

Way A — let SVG do it: <g transform="rotate(deg cx cy)">. Simple. Great when you just want a group to visually spin.

Way B — compute rotated coordinates yourself in JS (the formula above). More work — so why bother? Because the prototype needs the rotated (x,y) numbers before it feeds them into the isometric projection P(x,y,z). You can't project first and rotate the flat picture after — the depth would be wrong. Rotate in model space, then project. That ordering is the whole reason Way B exists here.
